## The real process

The engine modelled is a classic stationary, double-acting, slide-valve reciprocating engine with a throttle and a centrifugal governor.

1. Fire heats the boiler. Water in the closed boiler turns to steam, and the steam is under pressure (a gauge shows it; a safety valve lets steam escape if the pressure gets too high). Steam is now in the boiler, at boiler pressure.
2. Steam leaves the boiler by the steam pipe. The throttle valve sets how much steam gets through. Steam is now in the steam pipe and steam chest, at reduced or full pressure depending on the throttle.
3. The slide valve in the steam chest uncovers a port to ONE end of the cylinder, so steam enters that end. At the same moment the other end is joined to the exhaust. Steam is now at one end of the cylinder, pushing on the piston.
4. The steam pushes the piston along the cylinder. The piston rod carries the push to the crosshead, which slides between guides so the rod stays straight. Steam is now doing work and (after cutoff) expanding.
5. The connecting rod carries the push from the crosshead to the crank, which turns the crankshaft. The back-and-forth motion becomes rotation. The flywheel on the crankshaft evens out the turning and carries the crank past the dead centres.
6. The eccentric on the crankshaft moves the slide valve, so the valve swaps ends at the right moment, every stroke, with no one operating it. Steam now goes in at the other end of the cylinder, and the piston is pushed back the other way (double-acting). The steam from the first end is now being exhausted.
7. In some engines the steam is cut off early (cutoff), so it finishes the stroke by expanding. This uses less steam for the same job.
8. Exhaust steam leaves the cylinder: into the air (the "puffs") or into a condenser, where it turns back to water at low pressure. Steam is now spent: in the air, or condensed.
9. The centrifugal governor is driven by the engine. If the engine speeds up, the balls swing out and rise and a linkage closes the throttle (less steam). If it slows down, the balls drop and the throttle opens (more steam). Speed is held nearly steady, not perfectly.

## Common mistakes (used as wrong answers)

- The fire (or hot gases) pushes the piston directly (steam made in the boiler pushes it: facts 2, 3, 4).
- Steam pushes the piston only one way and something else pushes it back (in a double-acting engine steam is admitted alternately to each side: facts 12, 13).
- The flywheel adds power (it stores energy and smooths the motion and carries the crank past dead centre: fact 19).
- The governor is a brake (it cuts or increases the steam supply through the throttle: facts 28, 29, 30).
- The governor opens the throttle when the balls rise (balls rise when speed rises, and that closes it: fact 28).
- The valve is moved by hand, or by the governor, every stroke (the eccentric on the crankshaft does it automatically: fact 14).
- Cutting off steam early wastes it or loses power (it saves steam because the trapped steam keeps working as it expands: facts 20, 21, 22).
- Exhaust steam is wasted/lost in every engine (a condenser recovers it as water and raised efficiency: facts 23, 26, 27).
- The safety valve is for controlling speed or power (it only lets steam out when pressure gets too high: facts 6, 7).
- Steam engines are very efficient (low, a few percent early on, perhaps 12% best in the 1840s: facts 35, 36, 37).
- Water becomes only a little more voluminous as steam (about 1600 times at atmospheric pressure: fact 4).

## What-ifs for Try it yourself

- Open the throttle wider: more steam reaches the cylinder, so more force on the piston, so more power and the engine speeds up (facts 8, 9, 29; "the valve opens, increasing the force with which the piston is driven").
- Close the throttle: less steam, less power, slower (facts 8, 28).
- Add load: the engine slows (fact 31). Without a governor, speed keeps falling until you open the throttle by hand; take load off and it speeds up (inferred from facts 31, 32; no source states the no-governor case directly).
- Governor connected: speed stays nearly steady as load changes, but dips a little under load because speed must change before the governor acts (facts 29, 31, 32, 33).
- Governor disconnected: speed rises or falls with the load (inferred from facts 31, 32).
- Raise boiler pressure (more fire): more force on the piston, until the safety valve opens at its set pressure and the extra steam escapes (facts 6, 7).
- Condenser on/off, cutoff earlier/later: economy changes (steam used per stroke), not the basic action (facts 20 to 27).
The sandbox is a simple model (steady throttle gives roughly proportional force; the governor adjusts the throttle; load is a steady drag) and says so.

## Variations and disagreements to note

- Throttling vs cutoff governing: this engine's governor works the throttle (facts 8, 28). Corliss and many later engines let the governor change the cutoff instead, which gave closer speed control and used less steam (facts 22, 33). The lesson should say "in this engine".
- The slide valve is not simply opposite to the piston. Cressy says "the motion of the valve is opposite to that of the piston" in a simplified description; real D-valve engines set the eccentric ahead of the crank and use lap and lead (Tennant). The lesson should not show valve and piston as mirror images. This detail could not be quoted with a clean number from a source I read, so it is not stated as a fact.
- "Balls rise" vs "swing out": Wisconsin says "rotating masses will swing out to a larger radius"; Cressy and Cambridge say "balls fly outwards" and "climb". Same behaviour; on a Watt governor outward swing is also upward.
- Double-acting and exhaust: Cressy's small engine example and the Cambridge Lego engine are simplified (the Lego engine is single-acting and uses an exhaust valve). Do not copy their details into the double-acting diagram.
- Efficiency figures vary by engine and era and by what is counted (boiler included or not). Watt: "2% to 3%" (arXiv) vs "ca. 3%" (Chicago). Wikipedia (secondary only, uncited, [citation needed]) gives 1-10% for non-condensing engines and 10-20% for the best condensing engines; not used as a fact. The 1919 Nature figure is for a locomotive, drawbar work, boiler included.
- Fuel saving by the Corliss gear: ASME says about 30 percent fuel; Wikipedia says 30% less steam. Same order; the lesson should say "about 30%" and attribute to the ASME brochure.
- "Safety valve" and "pressure gauge" quotes are from a 1918 book and a heritage railway page; no museum or government page gave a clean explanation except the Kansas boiler rule (set at or below maximum allowable working pressure). Boiler pressures vary widely (Cressy's example uses 60 lb per square inch).
- The 1600x ratio is at 100 C and atmospheric pressure (fact 4). At boiler pressure the steam is denser, so the ratio is smaller; this is the author's inference, not a sourced figure, so the lesson should quote 1600x only for atmospheric pressure.
