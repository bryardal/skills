# Fact sheet: How does a sentence work? (subject, verb, object and how word order changes meaning) (kid level)

Checked 21 Oct 2026. Every line in the lesson maps to a fact below. Quotes are as returned by the fetch tool (asked
for word-for-word sentences); Cambridge Dictionary could not be fetched.

## The real process

1. A clause (a simple sentence) is built around a verb: the doing word.
2. The subject is the noun that does the verb. In English it comes before the verb.
3. The object is the noun that receives the action. In English it comes after the verb.
4. English uses the order subject, verb, object, so the order tells the reader who did what to whom.
5. Swap the subject and the object and the meaning changes: the other one now does the action.
6. Many other languages show who did what with word endings or prefixes instead, and the most common order across
   the world's languages puts the verb last (subject, object, verb).

## What varies or is debated

- English is not always subject, verb, object: questions and other sentence types change the order (fact 7 says
  "uses", 10 says "usually"). The lesson only uses simple statements.
- Languages differ in how much order matters (13, 14).

## Common mistakes (used as wrong answers)

- Swapping the names keeps the same meaning because the words are the same (contradicted by 9, 11).
- Any word after the verb is the object (Oak lists "Pupils may think any group of words following a verb is an
  object." as a misconception; used only lightly).
- Every language puts the verb in the middle like English (contradicted by 15, 17).

## What-ifs for Try it yourself

- Swap subject and object: the other one now does the action (8, 9, 11).
- Break the subject, verb, object order: the order no longer tells the reader who did what (6, 10). The sandbox says
  only that the order stops telling us; it does not claim the sentence is meaningless in every language (13).
