## The real process

1. Signals from other neurons reach the dendrites (and cell body) at synapses. Each input nudges the membrane voltage up (excitatory) or down (inhibitory).
2. Inputs add up (summation, in space and in time) where the dendrites and cell body meet the axon. The axon hillock (essentially the initial segment of the axon) is where the sum is "read".
3. At rest the inside of the cell is about -70 mV relative to the outside. The sodium-potassium pump keeps the sodium and potassium concentration differences that make this voltage.
4. If the sum at the axon's start reaches threshold (roughly -55 mV; sources give -50 to -60), voltage-gated sodium channels open. Sodium ions rush in and the inside becomes positive (peak about +30 mV or higher). If threshold is not reached, nothing fires.
5. At the peak the sodium channels close (inactivate) and voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium flows out, the voltage falls back (repolarization) and briefly dips below rest (undershoot) before returning to rest. The whole spike lasts about 1-2 ms.
6. The spike is regenerative: the voltage change at one patch of axon brings the next patch to threshold, so it travels along the axon toward the terminals. Just-fired patches are in a refractory period, so the spike moves forward, not back.
7. Myelin (made by oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord, Schwann cells elsewhere) insulates the axon. Spikes are made only at the gaps (nodes of Ranvier) and "jump" from node to node (saltatory conduction), which is much faster (up to about 150 m/s, against 0.5-10 m/s unmyelinated).
8. When the spike reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open. Vesicles fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft (exocytosis).
9. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft (tens of nanometres) and binds receptors on the next cell. In the simplest case the receptor is itself an ion channel and it opens, changing the next cell's voltage.
10. The signal ends when neurotransmitter is removed: taken back up by the terminal or by glial cells, broken down by enzymes, or it drifts away from the receptors.
11. Strength is coded by how often spikes fire, not by how big each one is: every spike is the same size.

## What varies or is debated

- All voltage numbers are typical values and vary by neuron type and species. Sources disagree: resting -70 (OS4) vs -75 mV (BRIT); threshold -50 (QBI) vs -60 mV (BRIT), with about -55 mV as the textbook middle; peak +30 (OS4) vs +55 mV (BRIT). The lesson should say "about" and "typical".
- Duration: about 1-2 ms is typical (OS4 about 2 ms; BRIT about 1 ms). Some neurons are slower.
- Speeds depend on axon width as well as myelin (OS4: wider axons conduct faster). 150 m/s is an upper limit, not typical.
- Cleft width: 20-40 nm (QBI) vs "less than 50 nm" (SP). Use "about 20-40 nm".
- Electrical synapses exist (fact 21); the lesson covers the chemical synapse only, and says so.
- Receptors that open ion channels directly (ionotropic) are the simple case; other receptors act more slowly through chemical messengers (OS5 mentions the ionotropic type only in what was returned).
- Where exactly summation "happens" (dendrites, soma, hillock) is simplified; sources give the initial segment as where the spike starts (fact 3).

## Common mistakes (used as wrong answers)

- "The signal is electricity flowing down the axon like a wire." Wrong: it is a self-regenerating voltage change made by ions crossing the membrane through channels (facts 5, 6, 8). Note: no source states "not like a wire" in so many words; this rests on facts 5-6.
- "The electrical signal jumps straight across the gap to the next cell." Wrong at a chemical synapse: neurotransmitter carries it across the cleft (facts 19, 21).
- "A stronger stimulus makes a bigger action potential." Wrong: all-or-none; strength changes how often (facts 11, 12).
- "Myelin is part of the neuron's own cell." Wrong: it is made by separate glial cells (oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells) wrapped around the axon (fact 16).
- "Sodium channels stay open until the signal is over." Wrong: they inactivate at the peak while potassium channels open (facts 7, 8).

## What-ifs for Try it yourself

- Stimulus below threshold: the voltage rises a little and fades; no action potential (facts 5, 11).
- Stimulus above threshold: a full-size spike (same height whatever the strength) (fact 11).
- Stronger stimulus: same-size spikes, more of them per second (fact 12). A limit exists because of the refractory period (fact 13).
- Myelin on: conduction is much faster (facts 14, 15). Myelin off: conduction is much slower (facts 14, 15). Heavy loss, as in multiple sclerosis, disrupts signalling (fact 17: "slows or blocks" not confirmed; say "disrupts" or "can slow", not "blocks").
