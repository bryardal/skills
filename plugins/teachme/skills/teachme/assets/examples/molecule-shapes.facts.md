# Fact sheet: Why is a water molecule bent? (student level)

Checked 24 Oct 2026. Every line in the lesson maps to a fact below.

Note on quotes: the fetch tool returned paraphrased/excerpted text, so every quote below is marked "(as returned by the fetch tool)": it was not checked word for word against the page. Several are fragments cut short by the tool.

## The real process

1. Each bond and each lone pair is a region of electrons around the central atom (O, N or C). Electrons repel each other.
2. The regions settle into the lowest-energy arrangement, spread out as far as they can be. Four regions point to the corners of a tetrahedron (angle 109.5 degrees).
3. Methane (CH4): four bonding pairs, no lone pair. The atoms sit at all four corners, so the shape is tetrahedral, H-C-H 109.5 degrees.
4. Ammonia (NH3): three bonding pairs and one lone pair. The lone pair is at one corner with no atom, so the three H atoms form a trigonal pyramid. A lone pair repels more than a bonding pair, so the H-N-H angle closes to about 107 degrees.
5. Water (H2O): two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. Only two corners hold atoms, so the shape is bent. The two lone pairs squeeze the angle to about 104.5 degrees.
6. The name of a molecule's shape counts atoms only. The electron-pair arrangement (all three are tetrahedral) counts lone pairs too.

## What varies or is debated

- The ammonia angle is quoted as 107 (McCord), 106.8 (OpenStax figure) and 106.7 (Averill figure). Use "about 107 degrees".
- McCord says the water "tweak" is "about 4-5 degrees" less; 109.5 minus 104.5 is 5 degrees. Fine at "about".
- VSEPR is a model that predicts shapes. The sources teach it as the rule; this sheet does not claim more.
- Bond lengths (O-H about 0.96 angstrom, C-H about 1.09, N-H about 1.01): NOT SOURCED within the fetch budget. One page checked (Wisconsin Chem 109) gave an O-H length of 94 pm (0.94 angstrom), which differs from 0.96, so do not put the 0.96 / 1.09 / 1.01 numbers in the lesson until a source is found. Leave bond length out, or draw all bonds without stated lengths.
- OpenStax says "regions", not "electron pairs" (a double or triple bond is one region). For this lesson all regions are single bonds or lone pairs, so "regions" and "electron pairs" are the same.
- Not obtained: Britannica (fetch failed), RSC and PubChem were not used.

## Common mistakes (used as wrong answers)

- Water is a straight line, H-O-H at 180 degrees (it is bent, about 104.5 degrees: facts 9, 11, 12).
- The angle in water is 90 degrees (it is 104.5 degrees, somewhat more than a right angle: facts 11, 12).
- Molecules are flat, like they are drawn on paper (they are three-dimensional; flat drawings mislead: fact 18; the tetrahedron: facts 3, 4).
- Lone pairs count as atoms in the shape, so water is "tetrahedral" (the shape name counts atoms only; water is bent: facts 9, 17).
- All three molecules have the same angle because they have the same four electron pairs (lone pairs repel more, so the angles shrink: facts 13, 15, 16).

## What-ifs for Try it yourself

Central atom with four electron pairs, choose the number of lone pairs.
- 0 lone pairs (4 bonding pairs, like methane): tetrahedral, 109.5 degrees (facts 3, 5, 6).
- 1 lone pair (3 bonding pairs, like ammonia): trigonal pyramidal, about 107 degrees (facts 7, 8, 13).
- 2 lone pairs (2 bonding pairs, like water): bent, about 104.5 degrees (facts 9, 10, 11, 13).
- More lone pairs squeeze the angle more (facts 15, 16).

## Numbers and how they were worked out

- 109.5 degrees is the tetrahedral angle (facts 3, 6). Worked out by us as a check, not from a source: the tetrahedral angle satisfies cos(angle) = -1/3, which gives 109.47 degrees.
- 109.5 - 107 = about 2.5 degrees squeezed by one lone pair; 109.5 - 104.5 = 5 degrees squeezed by two lone pairs. This is our subtraction from facts 6, 8, 11; sources only say "slightly less" and "4-5 degrees".
- Electron-pair counts (methane 4+0, ammonia 3+1, water 2+2) come from the AX4 / AX3E / AX2E2 notation and the OpenStax ammonia quote (facts 3, 7, 9). Each adds to four.
